Soccer: First Touch Before Speed

For young players, the game gets easier the moment the ball stops running away from them. A calm first touch — receiving the ball and bringing it under control — gives a child time to look up, choose a direction, and enjoy the next play. Speed matters later. Control comes first, and it is very coachable at ages 6 to 12.

Why First Touch Comes First at Ages 6–12

Watch a typical U8 or U10 game and you will see the same pattern: a pass arrives, the ball bounces two or three steps away, and the receiver has to chase it down. By the time they get there, the space they wanted is gone and another player is already close. The problem is rarely effort. It is that the first contact sent the ball too far from their feet.

A good first touch does the opposite. It kills the pace of the ball just enough and places it a short step away, where the player can protect it. That one small moment buys time — time to dribble, pass, or turn. When kids feel that control, they ask for the ball more. When every touch is a chase, they start hiding from passes without realizing it.

That is why we put receiving before speed work. Sprint drills have their place, but a fast player who cannot settle the ball still has to slow down to recover it. A player who can receive cleanly can play at whatever speed the game asks for. Touch first, and speed becomes useful instead of frantic.

The Body Shape That Makes Receiving Easier

Before the ball arrives, encourage two simple habits. The first is to receive side-on, not square to the ball. Standing a little sideways, with one shoulder pointing toward the incoming ball, lets a player see the ball and some of the field behind them. It also makes it easier to let the ball roll across the body or turn away in one motion.

The second habit is the quick shoulder check. A fast glance over the shoulder before the ball arrives answers a big question: is someone close behind me, or do I have space? For a 9-year-old, keep the language tiny: “Take a peek before it gets to you.” One glance is plenty.

When contact comes, think soft, not stiff. A locked ankle and a jabbing foot send the ball bouncing away. A relaxed foot that gives a little on contact cushions the ball, like catching an egg instead of swatting it. Then guide that first touch away from pressure — if a defender is on the right, take the touch to the left, into open grass. Soft, then away. Two words a young player can actually remember during a game.

Start Still, Then Add Movement

Progressions keep this fun instead of frustrating. Start stationary. Players stand still and receive a gentle toss or a soft rolled pass from a partner a few steps away. Success here looks like the ball stopping within one comfortable step. Celebrate that — it is the whole skill in its simplest form.

Next, add movement. The receiver jogs slowly toward the ball or moves sideways along a line before receiving. The body is moving, so timing matters more, but the goal is the same: the first touch stays close and points toward the next action. Keep the service kind and predictable. The challenge is the touch, not a wild ball.

Only after that feels steady do we add passive pressure: a defender who stands nearby or walks in slowly, without trying to win the ball. Their job is just to be a presence, so the receiver practices taking the touch away from them. If a step feels messy, drop back one level for a few reps. Going back is not failure — it is how young players lock in the good habit. You can find more receiving ideas organized by age in our soccer drills library.

Three Simple Drills That Build Control

You do not need fancy equipment. A ball for each pair, a few cones if you have them, and an age-appropriate ball — size 3 for the youngest players, size 4 for most 8- to 12-year-olds — will do fine.

Drill 1: Partner Rolls and Settle

  1. Setup: Two players face each other six to eight steps apart. One starts with the ball.
  2. How it runs: The server rolls the ball gently. The receiver takes one soft touch to stop it close, then rolls it back. Ten touches each, then switch.
  3. What to watch for: A relaxed foot, not a jab. The ball should finish within one step.

Drill 2: Gate Touch

  1. Setup: Make a small gate with two cones about a yard apart, behind the receiver.
  2. How it runs: The server rolls the ball in. The receiver’s first touch pushes it gently through the gate, then they turn and collect it.
  3. What to watch for: The touch has a direction, not just a stop. Look for the peek before the ball arrives.

Drill 3: Receive and Turn Away

  1. Setup: One server, one receiver, and one passive defender a couple of steps behind or beside the receiver.
  2. How it runs: The server passes to the receiver, who takes their first touch away from the defender and dribbles into space. The defender walks, never tackles. Rotate every five reps.
  3. What to watch for: The first touch moves away from pressure and the head comes up right after. If touches get heavy, remove the defender.

Small Games That Manufacture Touches

Drills teach the shape; small games are where kids get repetitions without standing in line. A 3v3 or 4v4 on a small field means every player is close to the action and the ball keeps coming back. A child might receive it dozens of times in ten minutes — far more than in a big game where the ball visits only once in a while.

Keep the field small enough that nobody can hide, and keep teams balanced so touches are shared. If one player is dominating, shrink the space or add a brief condition, such as everyone on a team touching the ball before a shot. Keep conditions short and positive. Rotate teams often; standing and listening is the enemy of touches at this age.

Best of all, first touch gets practiced at game speed without anyone calling it a drill. A player receives under a little pressure, takes a touch away, and plays on — the exact moment from the progressions, now on its own. Let them play, and coach in short moments between games.

What to Say When the Ball Bounces Away

It will bounce away — often, and for every kid on the team. What a 9-year-old hears in that moment shapes whether they want the next pass. Keep it to one cue at a time: “Soft foot,” or “Take it to the side,” or “Peek first.” One idea, said warmly, then let them try again right away.

Praise the attempt at control, not just the perfect result. “You tried to take that away from pressure — exactly right, the idea was there” tells a player their thinking was good and the feet will catch up. Kids repeat what gets noticed, and that keeps them brave enough to keep asking for the ball.

Finish with water nearby, regular breaks, and shin guards and ball size checked for the age group — the simple basics that keep practice safe. Send them home with one small win.
