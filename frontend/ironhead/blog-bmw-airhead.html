BMW Airhead Buying Guide: Picking the Right /5, /6, or /7

Choose the BMW generation by use and condition: /5s bring the earliest, most collectible character, /6s add a five-speed and strong road manners, and /7s offer refinement and touring equipment. Whatever the badge, inspect splines, seals, carbs, electrics, final drive, odometer wear, and insist on a fully warm test ride before buying.

This BMW airhead buying guide covers the air-cooled boxer twins BMW built from 1970 through the mid-'90s — the /5, /6, and /7 generations that turned "reliable motorcycle" from an oxymoron into a German export. Airheads are the thinking person's vintage bike: shaft drive, dead-simple valves you adjust with a wrench, and engineering that assumes the owner does their own work.

They're also bikes where condition matters more than mileage, thanks to the five-digit odometer. More on that shortly.

/5 vs /6 vs /7: Know What You're Looking At

/5 (1970–1973): R50/5, R60/5, R75/5. The originals. Four-speed gearbox, drum front brake on early bikes with a front disc arriving during the production run — verify the brake setup on each individual bike — and the famous "toaster" tank with chrome side panels. Most collectible of the three generations; the R75/5 with the toaster tank is the poster bike.

/6 (1974–1976): R60/6, R75/6, R90/6, and the legendary R90S. Five-speed gearbox, front disc brake standard, bigger tanks. The R90S — Daytona Orange or Silver Smoke, bikini fairing, 67 horsepower — is the one collectors fight over.

/7 (1977–1980): R60/7, R75/7, R80/7, R100/7, R100S, R100RS, R100RT. Refined and civilized: better electrics, the first factory full fairings (RS/RT), and cast "snowflake" wheels. Early snowflake wheels were subject to a recall, so confirm that the relevant update has been completed. The RS remains one of the great sport-touring bikes ever made.

Later airheads — the R80G/S, the monoshock R80 and R100 models through 1995 — are excellent motorcycles but a separate market. This guide sticks to the slash bikes.

BMW Airhead Buying Guide: The Inspection Checklist

Airheads are honest bikes. They show their maintenance history if you know where to look.

Splines. The transmission input splines need periodic lubrication, and skipped spline lubes kill transmissions. Ask when it was last done. A clunky-shifting bike with unknown spline history is a negotiation point, not a dealbreaker — but price the risk.

Rear main seal. Oil weeping from the bottom of the bell housing means the rear main seal is going. The seal itself is cheap; getting to it means pulling the transmission. Common, fixable, and worth a discount.

Pushrod tube seals. They weep. They all weep eventually. Light seepage is a maintenance item; heavy leaking means top-end attention.

Carbs. Bing CVs are simple and long-lived, but the rubber diaphragms crack with age. If the bike stumbles off idle or won't hold a steady cruise, budget for carb kits. Check that the choke (enrichener) cables move freely.

Electrics. The diode board and its rubber mounts, the ground connections, the starter relay. Airhead electrics are simple but ground-dependent — corroded grounds cause half the "mystery" problems. A bike that starts crisply and charges at 13.5+ volts at cruising rpm is a healthy bike.

Odometer honesty. Five-digit odometers. That "28,000-mile" /5 may have 128,000 miles. This is why service records, overall condition, and wear points (footpeg rubbers, grips, seat) matter more than the number in the window. Judge the bike, not the digits.

Final drive and forks. Check the final drive for leaks and notchiness, fork seals for weeping, and the sidestand — early stands are famously eager to retract themselves. Ask the seller to demonstrate the centerstand; a bike that's lived on its sidestand for a decade tells you about its owner.

What to Pay

Treat these as ballpark figures and verify them against recent sales: a scruffy-but-running /6 or /7 typically sits in the low-to-mid single thousands; clean, documented riders climb from there; toaster-tank /5s and R90Ss command real premiums — the R90S in particular lives in its own market. Unrestored originals with records beat shiny unknowns at the same price, every time.

The first time I rode a friend's R75/5, I finally understood why grown adults describe a motorcycle as "a tractor" with genuine affection. It wasn't fast. It was right.

One more buying tip that applies to every airhead: ride it before you buy it, and ride it long enough to get it fully warm — at least 20 minutes. Airheads hide their sins when cold. Weeping seals, tired carbs, and weak charging all show up once the engine is at operating temperature. A seller who won't allow a proper warm test ride is telling you something, and it's not about the bike.

Parts, Community, and Why Airheads Are Safe Bets

Parts support for airheads is among the best in vintage motorcycling — new, used, and reproduction parts are all available, and the owner community documents everything. These are bikes designed to be maintained by their owners, with factory manuals that assume you're competent. You'll never lack for guidance.

Buyer questions

Questions worth asking before you buy

Which BMW airhead generation is best for a first-time buyer?

A well-documented /6 or /7 is usually the most approachable choice because parts, upgrades, and riding equipment are plentiful. A /5 has more collectible character, but condition and history matter more than the generation badge.

What should I check first when inspecting a BMW airhead?

Start with service records and the transmission spline history, then check seals, carburetors, charging voltage, final drive, forks, and wear points that reveal true mileage. Always inspect the bike cold and ride it until fully warm.

How important is a test ride on a BMW airhead?

It is essential. A warm ride can reveal charging weakness, tired carburetors, slipping or clunky shifting, and leaks that a cold static inspection can hide.
