Honda CB750 Buying Guide: From Sandcast K0 to K8

Choose a CB750 K0–K8 by deciding whether you want collectible rarity or an honest rider: K0 is the sandcast premium, K1–K2 offer early appeal, and K3–K8 are practical values. Verify VIN and originality, then inspect exhaust, tank, carbs, wiring, wheels, cam-chain noise, and test-ride the bike fully.

Any Honda CB750 buying guide has to acknowledge what the bike is: the machine that invented the modern superbike. When the CB750 Four landed in 1969 — four cylinders, disc brake, 120 mph, Honda reliability — it made everything else on the showroom floor look like a museum piece. The K-series ran through 1978, and today it's the most approachable classic Japanese superbike you can buy.

My first bike was a 1973 Kawasaki KZ900, bought in 1974 when I was a high school senior — the CB750's great rival. I have loved and respected the CB750 ever since, in the way you respect the opponent who made you better.

K0 to K8 in Sixty Seconds

  • K0 (1969): The sandcast. Rough sand-cast engine cases, first-year everything. The collectible — worth multiples of any other K model.
  • K1–K2 (1970–1972): Die-cast cases, refinements. The sweet spot for riders who want the early look without the sandcast premium.
  • K3–K6 (1973–1976): Ongoing evolution — colors, details, emissions bits. Good honest motorcycles, sanest prices.
  • F models (1975–1976 F1, 1977 F2/F3): The Super Sport with the 4-into-1 exhaust, rearsets-ish ergonomics, and café style from the factory.
  • K7–K8 (1977–1978): The last of the single-cam line, with the F-model engine updates. Often overlooked, often a bargain.

Sandcast vs Die-Cast: Why K0 Costs What It Costs

The first ~7,400 CB750s had engine cases cast in sand molds, leaving a rough, pebbled finish. Honda switched to die-cast cases early in production. The sandcasts are identifiable by VIN range (starting CB750-1000001) and that unmistakable case texture. They're the 1969 Camaro Z/28 of Japanese bikes — the one everyone wants, priced accordingly.

If someone offers you a "sandcast" at a K2 price, it's either the deal of your life or a lie. Verify the VIN range and learn the case texture before you believe it.

Numbers and Originality

CB750 frame numbers (CB750-1XXXXXX) and engine numbers (CB750E-1XXXXXX) run in sequence but were never "matching" in the Harley sense — close numbers suggest the original pairing, and that's what you want to see. Re-stamped cases exist in the premium market; compare stamp fonts and pad surfaces against known-good examples, and be extra careful on anything priced as a K0.

Originality checklist, in order of value impact: original 4-into-4 exhaust (worth serious money alone), correct seat and side covers, original gauges, uncut wiring harness, original airbox (pod filters are a red flag for hackery), and correct paint. A complete, original bike beats a "restored" one with reproduction everything.

Honda CB750 Buying Guide: The Inspection List

Cam chain tensioner. The single overhead cam is driven by a chain with a manual tensioner. A rattle on startup that doesn't quiet down means adjustment or wear. It's a known maintenance item, not a death sentence — but it should be reflected in the price.

Carbs. Four Keihins that need to agree with each other. If the bike idles rough, stumbles, or the seller says "just needs the carbs cleaned" — budget for a full carb service with kits, and assume the tank has opinions too.

Tank rust. Pull the cap and look inside with a flashlight. Surface rust is treatable; flaking rust that has been feeding the carbs means tank work plus carb work. Check the petcock flows cleanly.

Spokes and wheels. Original spokes corrode; check for broken or replaced spokes and true wheels. Stainless re-spokes are a plus on a rider.

Electrics. Uncut harness, working charging system, all lights and signals functional. The CB750's electrics are simple — which makes hacked wiring inexcusable.

The test ride. It should start easily, idle evenly, pull cleanly through the revs, shift without drama, and stop straight. These bikes are famously robust; a sorted one feels almost modern. If it feels tired, it is tired.

Fair Prices

Verify against recent sales, but the shape of the market is stable: K3–K8 riders in decent shape sit in the low-to-mid single thousands; clean K1–K2 bikes climb from there; F models carry a style premium; and sandcast K0s start at multiples of everything else and go up with originality. As always, buy the most original, best-documented bike your budget allows — restoration costs more than purchase premium, without exception.

The Parts Situation Is Excellent

The CB750 might have the best vintage parts support of any Japanese bike: reproduction bodywork, exhausts, gaskets, carb kits, cables — nearly everything is available. The owner community is huge and the knowledge base is deep. This is a genuinely practical classic to own and ride.

Buyer questions

Questions worth asking before you buy

How should I choose between a Honda CB750 K0 through K8?

Choose a K0 for rarity and collectibility, a K1 or K2 for early-bike appeal with a lower premium, or a K3 through K8 for a practical rider and saner pricing. F models are a separate style-driven choice.

How can I verify that a CB750 is an original sandcast K0?

Verify the VIN range beginning CB750-1000001 and inspect the rough, pebbled sandcast case texture. Compare stamp fonts and pad surfaces with known-good examples because re-stamped cases exist.

What should a CB750 test ride tell me?

The bike should start easily, idle evenly, pull cleanly through the revs, shift without drama, and stop straight. A test ride also helps expose carb, cam-chain, clutch, and charging issues that a static inspection can miss.
