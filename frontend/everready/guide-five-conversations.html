The Conversations: Before and After

The papers matter. But before the papers, somebody has to talk. And after the papers are signed, somebody still has to talk. Most families skip both — not because they don't care, but because nobody knows how to start.

I was the executor of my mother's estate. Here's what I learned about the conversations before, and the ones after.

Before

1. Talking with your dying parent

Why it's hard: It feels like giving up — or like you're after the money. So everybody waits, and then it's too late, and you're guessing.

The reframe: This isn't about death. It's about honoring them. "I want to do this the way you'd want it done" changes the whole conversation.

When: Sooner than you think. A calm afternoon, not a crisis. If you're waiting for the right moment, this is it.

How to start (say it plain):

  • "Mom, I want to make sure I honor your wishes. Can we talk through the practical stuff, just so I get it right?"
  • "Dad, if something happened tomorrow, I wouldn't know where to start. Will you walk me through it?"

What to cover:

  • Where the will is — and that it's the signed original, not a copy.
  • The accounts, the insurance, the passwords. All of it, in one list.
  • Any debts. Surprises after the funeral are the worst kind.
  • Who gets the personal things that actually matter — and why. The "why" prevents fights.
  • Funeral and burial wishes. Music, readings, who speaks. Write it down while they can tell you.
  • Who they trust to make decisions if they can't. Name the person, get the paperwork.

What not to do:

  • Don't bring the whole family as an audience the first time. One-on-one is honest; a crowd is a performance.
  • Don't argue about fairness while they're alive to hear it. That's a conversation for later, without them in the room.
  • Don't record them secretly. Ask. Most parents are relieved to be asked.

The part everyone skips: Ask for the stories. "What's the story behind this?" The watch, the quilt, the dented toolbox — the story is the real inheritance, and it's the one thing the will can't transfer.

2. Talking with your siblings — before

Why it's hard: You're all adults with old roles. The responsible one, the distant one, the one who never calls. Money makes every old wound louder.

The reframe: This is logistics, not therapy. You're agreeing on a plan while everyone is calm, so nobody has to improvise while grieving.

How to start:

  • "While Mom and Dad can still tell us what they want, let's get on the same page. One call, thirty minutes."
  • "I don't want us guessing later. Can we agree now on how we'll handle things?"

What to cover:

  • Who's the executor — and does that person actually want the job? Name a backup.
  • What the parents' wishes are, in their own words. Write them down where everyone can see them.
  • Who does what: one person handles the house, one handles the paperwork, one handles the people calling. Divide it now.
  • The personal property question, early and calmly: "Let's each make a list of the five things that matter most to us, and we'll trade from there." Done before grief arrives, it's easy. Done after, it's war.
  • Agree on the rule now: nothing leaves the house until the family talks. One sentence, agreed in advance, prevents the 2 a.m. U-Haul.

What not to do:

  • Don't let one sibling become the sole keeper of information. Everything important goes to everyone, in writing.
  • Don't assume the closest sibling should do everything. Closest usually means most exhausted.
  • Don't put it in a group text. Say it out loud, together, once.

After

3. Talking with your siblings — after

Why it's hard: You're grieving, you're tired, and now you have to make decisions with the same people you fought with over the back seat in 1978.

The reframe: Your parent's last gift to you was the plan. Your job is to carry it out the way they'd be proud of.

The first meeting — within two weeks:

  • Everybody together, in person or on video. No spouses the first time if that's cleaner — this is sibling business.
  • The executor runs it. Read the will together, out loud. No surprises later.
  • Set the timeline: what happens in 30 days, 60 days, 90 days.
  • Divide the work the way you agreed before — or divide it now, fast, before resentment picks the assignments for you.

Dividing the things:

  • Use the lists from before. Take turns choosing, or trade. Slow is fine; secret is not.
  • The fairness trap: equal isn't always fair, and fair isn't always equal. The will decides — not the loudest sibling, not the one who visited most.
  • What gets sold: agree on how (estate sale, auction, family bidding), agree on who handles it, agree that proceeds split per the will. Get it in writing, even among family. Especially among family.

What not to do:

  • No side deals. No "Mom promised me this" without proof. If it isn't in the will or the written wishes, it's a family discussion, not a claim.
  • Nothing leaves the house before the talk. Remember the rule.
  • Don't let silence decide. The sibling who won't talk isn't agreeing — they're building a lawsuit.

If it breaks: A mediator — often the estate attorney — costs a fraction of a lawsuit and doesn't ruin Thanksgiving forever. Bringing one in isn't failure. It's the executor doing the job.

4. Talking with your heirs — your kids

Why it's hard: You don't want to scare them. And honestly, you don't want to think about it either.

The reframe: This is a gift, not a burden. "I'm telling you this so you never have to guess" is the whole speech.

How to start:

  • "If something happened to me, here's the drawer, and here's the person to call. That's it. Two minutes."
  • Keep it plain and short. Not a lecture. Not a tour of your mortality. Two minutes.

What to tell them:

  • Where the one drawer is — the will, the insurance, the account list, the passwords.
  • Who the executor is, and that person's phone number.
  • The big wishes: the house, burial or cremation, the service. The things they'd otherwise argue about.
  • That it's all written down, and where "written down" lives.

The letter: Write one page. Not legal — human. What you want them to know that isn't in the will: what you were proud of, what you hope for them, the story only you can tell. The will divides things. The letter explains you.

Keep it current: When life changes — a move, a new account, a new grandchild — update the drawer and tell them you did. "Updated the drawer" is a five-second text that saves them five weeks.

The whole cause, in one paragraph

One drawer. One conversation before, one conversation after. That's it. The families who talk — plainly, early, out loud — don't end up in court, and they don't end up estranged. They end up grieving together instead of fighting each other. That's what we're building here.
